# Exponential and Logarithmic Functions

The study of the properties of powers, exponential and logarithmic functions, the inverse relationship between them, the natural number $e$, logarithmic scales (such as the Richter scale), and logarithmic graphs.

---

## 1. Exponential Functions

An exponential function has the variable in the exponent and a constant base.

### Definition of an Exponential Function
The exponential function with base $a$ is defined as:
$$
f(x) = a^x
$$
Where $a > 0$ and $a \neq 1$.
- **Domain:** $D(f) = \mathbb{R}$
- **Range:** $R(f) = (0, \infty)$

### Properties of Exponential Functions
- If $a > 1$, the function is strictly increasing. As $x \to \infty$, $a^x \to \infty$, while as $x \to -\infty$, $a^x \to 0$ (horizontal asymptote $y = 0$).
- If $0 < a < 1$, the function is strictly decreasing. As $x \to \infty$, $a^x \to 0$, while as $x \to -\infty$, $a^x \to \infty$.
- For every base $a$, $a^0 = 1$ holds (the graph always passes through the point $(0,1)$).

### The Natural Number $e$
The most important base in mathematics is the irrational number $e$, which is defined as the limit:
$$
e = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n \approx 2.71828
$$
The function $f(x) = e^x$ is called the natural exponential function.

---

## 2. Logarithmic Functions

The logarithmic function is the inverse of the exponential function.

### Definition of Logarithm
For $a > 0$ and $a \neq 1$, the logarithm of $x$ with base $a$ is denoted by $\log_a(x)$ and is defined as:
$$
y = \log_a(x) \iff a^y = x
$$
- **Domain:** $D(\log_a) = (0, \infty)$
- **Range:** $R(\log_a) = \mathbb{R}$

### Inverse Relationship
Since the exponential and logarithmic functions are inverses of each other, the following cancellation relations hold:
$$
\log_a(a^x) = x, \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{R}
$$
$$
a^{\log_a(x)} = x, \quad \forall x > 0
$$

### Basic Properties of Logarithms
For any $x, y > 0$:
1. **Logarithm of a Product:** $\log_a(x \cdot y) = \log_a(x) + \log_a(y)$
2. **Logarithm of a Quotient:** $\log_a\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \log_a(x) - \log_a(y)$
3. **Logarithm of a Power:** $\log_a(x^r) = r \cdot \log_a(x)$
4. **Change of Base:** $\log_b(x) = \frac{\log_a(x)}{\log_a(b)}$
5. **Natural Logarithm:** When the base is $e$, we write $\ln(x)$ instead of $\log_e(x)$.

---

## 3. Logarithmic Scales and Graphs

Logarithmic scales are used to represent quantities that vary over an enormous range of values.

### Richter Scale
The magnitude $M$ of an earthquake is defined logarithmically in terms of the energy $E$ released:
$$
M = \frac{2}{3} \log_{10}\left(\frac{E}{E_0}\right)
$$
Where $E_0$ is a reference minimum energy. A difference of $1$ unit on the Richter scale corresponds to a multiplication of the energy by $10^{1.5} \approx 31.6$ times.

## Solved Exercises

### Exercise 1: Solving an Exponential Equation
**Problem:**
Solve the equation:
$$
3^{x+2} = 9^{x-1}
$$

**Solution:**
1. Express both sides of the equation with the same base (base $3$):
   $$
   9 = 3^2 \implies 9^{x-1} = (3^2)^{x-1} = 3^{2(x-1)} = 3^{2x-2}
   $$
2. Equate the exponents due to the uniqueness (1-1 property) of the exponential function:
   $$
   x + 2 = 2x - 2
   $$
3. Solve for $x$:
   $$
   2 + 2 = 2x - x \implies x = 4
   $$

### Exercise 2: Solving a Logarithmic Equation
**Problem:**
Solve the equation:
$$
\log_2(x) + \log_2(x - 2) = 3
$$

**Solution:**
1. **Restrictions (Domain):** The arguments of the logarithms must be strictly positive:
   $$
   x > 0 \quad \text{and} \quad x - 2 > 0 \implies x > 2
   $$
   Therefore, any acceptable solutions must belong to the interval $(2, \infty)$.
2. We use the property of the sum of logarithms:
   $$
   \log_2(x(x - 2)) = 3
   $$
3. Convert the logarithmic equation to exponential form:
   $$
   x(x - 2) = 2^3 \implies x^2 - 2x = 8 \implies x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0
   $$
4. Solve the quadratic trinomial:
   $$
   (x - 4)(x + 2) = 0 \implies x = 4 \quad \text{or} \quad x = -2
   $$
5. Check the restrictions: the solution $x = 4$ is accepted since $4 > 2$, while $x = -2$ is rejected.

### Exercise 3: Graphing via Transformation
**Problem:**
Describe how the graph of $y = 2 - e^{-x}$ is obtained from the basic graph $y = e^x$.

**Solution:**
1. We start from the natural exponential function $y = e^x$.
2. $y = e^{-x}$ is obtained by reflecting across the vertical axis $y'y$.
3. $y = -e^{-x}$ is obtained by reflecting the previous graph across the horizontal axis $x'x$.
4. $y = 2 - e^{-x}$ (or $-e^{-x} + 2$) is obtained by a vertical translation of $2$ units upward.
5. The horizontal asymptote shifts from $y = 0$ to $y = 2$.

### Exercise 4: Finding the Domain of a Logarithm
**Problem:**
Find the domain of the function:
$$
f(x) = \ln(x^2 - x - 6)
$$

**Solution:**
1. For the natural logarithm to be defined, its argument must be positive:
   $$
   x^2 - x - 6 > 0
   $$
2. We find the roots of the trinomial $x^2 - x - 6 = 0$:
   $$
   (x - 3)(x + 2) = 0 \implies x = 3 \quad \text{or} \quad x = -2
   $$
3. The trinomial is positive (having the same sign as $a=1 > 0$) outside the interval of its roots. Consequently:
   $$
   x \in (-\infty, -2) \cup (3, \infty)
   $$
4. The domain is:
   $$
   D(f) = (-\infty, -2) \cup (3, \infty)
   $$

### Exercise 5: Proving a Logarithm Property
**Problem:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> Prove the property: $\log_a(x \cdot y) = \log_a(x) + \log_a(y)$ for $x, y > 0$.

**Solution:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> 1. Set $u = \log_a(x)$ and $v = \log_a(y)$.
> 2. From the definition of the logarithm, this means:
>    $$
>    x = a^u \quad \text{and} \quad y = a^v
>    $$
> 3. We multiply the two expressions:
>    $$
>    x \cdot y = a^u \cdot a^v = a^{u+v}
>    $$
> 4. We apply the definition of the logarithm with base $a$ to the above equality:
>    $$
>    \log_a(x \cdot y) = u + v
>    $$
> 5. We substitute $u$ and $v$ back with their original expressions:
>    $$
>    \log_a(x \cdot y) = \log_a(x) + \log_a(y)
>    $$

### Exercise 6: Finding the Inverse with Exponential Terms
**Problem:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> Find the inverse function of:
> $$
> f(x) = \frac{e^x - e^{-x}}{e^x + e^{-x}}
> $$

**Solution:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> 1. Set $y = \frac{e^x - e^{-x}}{e^x + e^{-x}}$ and solve for $x$.
> 2. Multiply the numerator and denominator by $e^x$ to simplify the $e^{-x}$ terms:
>    $$
>    y = \frac{e^{2x} - 1}{e^{2x} + 1}
>    $$
> 3. Cross-multiply:
>    $$
>    y(e^{2x} + 1) = e^{2x} - 1 \implies y e^{2x} + y = e^{2x} - 1 \implies y + 1 = e^{2x}(1 - y)
>    $$
> 4. Solve for $e^{2x}$:
>    $$
>    e^{2x} = \frac{1 + y}{1 - y}
>    $$
>    It is required that $\frac{1 + y}{1 - y} > 0 \implies y \in (-1, 1)$.
> 5. Take the natural logarithm:
>    $$
>    2x = \ln\left(\frac{1 + y}{1 - y}\right) \implies x = \frac{1}{2} \ln\left(\frac{1 + y}{1 - y}\right)
>    $$
> 6. The inverse function is:
>    $$
>    f^{-1}(x) = \frac{1}{2} \ln\left(\frac{1 + x}{1 - x}\right)
>    $$
>    with domain $D(f^{-1}) = (-1, 1)$.

### Exercise 7: Seismological Application
**Problem:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> Earthquake $A$ has a magnitude of $8.0$ on the Richter scale, while earthquake $B$ has a magnitude of $6.0$. How many times greater energy did earthquake $A$ release compared to earthquake $B$?

**Solution:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> 1. We use the magnitude-energy relation:
>    $$
>    M = \frac{2}{3} \log_{10}\left(\frac{E}{E_0}\right) \implies \log_{10}\left(\frac{E}{E_0}\right) = 1.5 M \implies E = E_0 \cdot 10^{1.5 M}
>    $$
> 2. Write the energy for the two earthquakes:
>    $$
>    E_A = E_0 \cdot 10^{1.5(8.0)} = E_0 \cdot 10^{12}
>    $$
>    $$
>    E_B = E_0 \cdot 10^{1.5(6.0)} = E_0 \cdot 10^{9}
>    $$
> 3. Divide the two energies term by term:
>    $$
>    \frac{E_A}{E_B} = \frac{E_0 \cdot 10^{12}}{E_0 \cdot 10^{9}} = 10^{12 - 9} = 10^3 = 1000
>    $$
> 4. Earthquake $A$ released $1000$ times more energy than earthquake $B$.

### Exercise 8: Plotting $e^x$ and $\ln(x)$ in Octave
**Problem:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> Write Octave code that plots $y = e^x$ and $y = \ln(x)$ on the same graph, together with the bisector $y = x$, showing their symmetry.

**Solution:**
> **[Supplementary]**
```octave
% Definition of intervals
x1 = -2:0.05:2;
x2 = 0.01:0.05:4;

% Computation of values
y1 = exp(x1);
y2 = log(x2);
y3 = x2; % bisector

% Plotting
figure;
plot(x1, y1, 'r-', 'LineWidth', 1.5); hold on;
plot(x2, y2, 'b-', 'LineWidth', 1.5);
plot(x2, y3, 'k--', 'LineWidth', 1);
hold off;

% Graph settings
grid on;
axis equal; % ensures correct geometric depiction of symmetry
xlim([-2, 4]);
ylim([-2, 4]);
legend('y = e^x', 'y = ln(x)', 'y = x', 'Location', 'NorthWest');
xlabel('x');
ylabel('y');
title('Symmetry of Exponential and Natural Logarithm Functions');
```

## Exam Tip: Simplifying Exponentials and Logarithms

In exponential and logarithmic equations, always try to isolate the exponential or logarithmic term on one side.
- If you have $a^{g(x)} = b$, take the logarithm of both sides: $g(x) = \log_a(b) = \frac{\ln(b)}{\ln(a)}$.
- If you have $\log_a(g(x)) = b$, raise the base to the power $b$: $g(x) = a^b$.
Before giving the final answer in logarithmic equations, **always check** whether the solutions you found make the arguments of the original logarithms strictly positive ($>0$). "Spurious" solutions that arise during the process are a very common trap in exams!
