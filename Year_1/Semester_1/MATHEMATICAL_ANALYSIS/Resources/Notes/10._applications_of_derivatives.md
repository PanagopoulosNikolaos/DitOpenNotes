# Applications of Derivatives

Study of the applications of the derivative in analyzing the monotonicity and extrema of a function, concavity and inflection points, linear approximation and differentials, and the de L'Hôpital rules for computing limits.

---

## 1. Monotonicity and Extrema

The first derivative provides information about the direction of the function (whether it is increasing or decreasing).

### Monotonicity Criterion
Let $f$ be a function continuous on an interval $I$ and differentiable on the interior of $I$:
- If $f'(x) > 0$ for every interior point $x$ of $I$, then $f$ is **strictly increasing** on $I$.
- If $f'(x) < 0$ for every interior point $x$ of $I$, then $f$ is **strictly decreasing** on $I$.

### Critical Points
An interior point $c$ of the domain of $f$ is called a critical point if:
$$
f'(c) = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad f'(c) \; \text{is not defined}
$$
*According to Fermat's theorem, local extrema of a function occur only at critical points or at the endpoints of the interval.*

---

## 2. Concavity and Inflection Points

The second derivative shows how the curve bends.

### Definition of Concavity
Let $f$ be a function differentiable on an interval $I$:
- The graph of $f$ is **concave up** (bends upward) on $I$ if $f'$ is strictly increasing. This occurs when $f''(x) > 0$.
- The graph of $f$ is **concave down** (bends downward) on $I$ if $f'$ is strictly decreasing. This occurs when $f''(x) < 0$.

### Inflection Point
A point $P(c, f(c))$ on the graph of a continuous function is called an inflection point if the curve changes concavity direction (from concave up to concave down or vice versa) on either side of $c$. At this point, typically $f''(c) = 0$ or $f''(c)$ does not exist.

---

## 3. Linear Approximation and Differentials

Linear approximation uses the tangent line to approximate function values near a known point.

### Linearization
The linearization of $f$ at $a$ is the tangent line function:
$$
L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)
$$
For values of $x$ close to $a$, the approximation holds: $f(x) \approx L(x)$.

---

## 4. de L'Hôpital Rules

Used to resolve indeterminate forms in limits.

### L'Hôpital's Theorem
Let $f, g$ be differentiable functions in a neighborhood of $x_0$ (possibly excluding $x_0$) and $\lim_{x \to x_0} f(x) = \lim_{x \to x_0} g(x) = 0$ or $\pm\infty$. If the limit of the derivatives exists, then:
$$
\lim_{x \to x_0} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to x_0} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}
$$
*The rule also applies when $x_0$ is $+\infty$ or $-\infty$.*

## Solved Exercises

### Exercise 1: Study of Monotonicity and Local Extrema
**Problem:**
Find the intervals of monotonicity and the local extrema of the function:
$$
f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 + 2
$$

**Solution:**
1. $f$ is defined on $\mathbb{R}$ with derivative:
   $$
   f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x = 3x(x - 2)
   $$
2. Find the roots of the derivative for the critical points:
   $$
   3x(x - 2) = 0 \implies x = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad x = 2
   $$
3. Study the sign of $f'(x)$:
   - On $(-\infty, 0)$: $f'(x) > 0 \implies f$ strictly increasing.
   - On $(0, 2)$: $f'(x) < 0 \implies f$ strictly decreasing.
   - On $(2, \infty)$: $f'(x) > 0 \implies f$ strictly increasing.
4. Determine the local extrema:
   - At $x = 0$ monotonicity changes from increasing to decreasing, so we have a **local maximum** with value $f(0) = 2$.
   - At $x = 2$ monotonicity changes from decreasing to increasing, so we have a **local minimum** with value $f(2) = 2^3 - 3(2^2) + 2 = -2$.

### Exercise 2: Global Extrema on a Closed Interval
**Problem:**
Find the global extrema of $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$ on the closed interval $[0, 2]$.

**Solution:**
1. The function is continuous on the closed interval $[0, 2]$.
2. Compute the derivative:
   $$
   f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x^2 - 1) = 3(x - 1)(x + 1)
   $$
3. Find the critical points in the interior of the interval $(0, 2)$:
   - The roots are $x = 1$ and $x = -1$.
   - Only $x = 1$ belongs to $(0, 2)$.
4. Compute $f$ values at the endpoints and the interior critical point:
   - $f(0) = 0^3 - 3(0) = 0$
   - $f(1) = 1^3 - 3(1) = -2$
   - $f(2) = 2^3 - 3(2) = 8 - 6 = 2$
5. Compare the values:
   - The minimum value is $-2$, occurring at $x = 1$ (**global minimum**).
   - The maximum value is $2$, occurring at $x = 2$ (**global maximum**).

### Exercise 3: Concavity and Inflection Points
**Problem:**
Determine the intervals of concavity and the inflection points of the function:
$$
f(x) = x e^{-x}
$$

**Solution:**
1. Compute the first derivative:
   $$
   f'(x) = (x)' e^{-x} + x (e^{-x})' = e^{-x} - x e^{-x} = e^{-x}(1 - x)
   $$
2. Compute the second derivative $f''(x)$:
   $$
   f''(x) = (e^{-x})'(1 - x) + e^{-x}(1 - x)' = -e^{-x}(1 - x) - e^{-x} = e^{-x}(x - 2)
   $$
3. Find the root of the second derivative:
   $$
   f''(x) = 0 \implies e^{-x}(x - 2) = 0 \implies x = 2 \quad (\text{since } e^{-x} > 0)
   $$
4. Study the sign of $f''(x)$:
   - For $x < 2$: $f''(x) < 0 \implies f$ concave down.
   - For $x > 2$: $f''(x) > 0 \implies f$ concave up.
5. Since concavity changes at $x = 2$, the point $P(2, 2e^{-2})$ is an **inflection point**.

### Exercise 4: Linear Approximation
**Problem:**
Find the linearization of $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ at $a = 4$ and use it to approximate $\sqrt{4.1}$.

**Solution:**
1. Compute the function value at $a = 4$:
   $$
   f(4) = \sqrt{4} = 2
   $$
2. Compute the derivative $f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$ and the slope at $a = 4$:
   $$
   f'(4) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{4}} = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25
   $$
3. Write the linearization $L(x)$:
   $$
   L(x) = f(4) + f'(4)(x - 4) = 2 + 0.25(x - 4)
   $$
4. Approximate the value at $x = 4.1$:
   $$
   f(4.1) \approx L(4.1) = 2 + 0.25(4.1 - 4) = 2 + 0.25(0.1) = 2 + 0.025 = 2.025
   $$
   *(The exact value from a calculator is $\sqrt{4.1} \approx 2.02484$, so the approximation is extremely accurate).*

### Exercise 5: L'Hôpital's Rule (0/0 Form)
**Problem:**
Compute the limit:
$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^x - 1 - x}{x^2}
$$

**Solution:**
1. Direct substitution gives $\frac{0}{0}$.
2. Apply L'Hôpital's rule by differentiating the numerator and denominator separately:
   $$
   \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\frac{d}{dx}[e^x - 1 - x]}{\frac{d}{dx}[x^2]} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^x - 1}{2x}
   $$
3. Substitution again gives the indeterminate form $\frac{0}{0}$.
4. Apply L'Hôpital's rule a second time:
   $$
   \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\frac{d}{dx}[e^x - 1]}{\frac{d}{dx}[2x]} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^x}{2}
   $$
5. Compute the limit by substitution:
   $$
   \frac{e^0}{2} = \frac{1}{2}
   $$

### Exercise 6: L'Hôpital's Rule for $0 \cdot \infty$ Form
**Problem:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> Compute the limit:
> $$
> \lim_{x \to 0^+} x \ln(x)
> $$

**Solution:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> 1. The limit is of the form $0 \cdot (-\infty)$.
> 2. Transform the expression to obtain the form $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ by moving $x$ to the denominator:
>    $$
>    x \ln(x) = \frac{\ln(x)}{\frac{1}{x}}
>    $$
> 3. Apply L'Hôpital's rule:
>    $$
>    \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\ln(x)}{\frac{1}{x}} = \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{(\ln(x))'}{(\frac{1}{x})'} = \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\frac{1}{x}}{-\frac{1}{x^2}}
>    $$
> 4. Simplify the complex fraction:
>    $$
>    \lim_{x \to 0^+} \left( -\frac{x^2}{x} \right) = \lim_{x \to 0^+} (-x) = 0
>    $$

### Exercise 7: L'Hôpital's Rule for $\infty - \infty$ Form
**Problem:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> Compute the limit:
> $$
> \lim_{x \to 0} \left( \frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{e^x - 1} \right)
> $$

**Solution:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> 1. The limit is of the form $\infty - \infty$.
> 2. Combine the fractions to create a single fraction of the form $\frac{0}{0}$:
>    $$
>    \frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{e^x - 1} = \frac{e^x - 1 - x}{x(e^x - 1)}
>    $$
> 3. Apply L'Hôpital's rule:
>    $$
>    \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^x - 1}{(e^x - 1) + x e^x}
>    $$
> 4. Substitution again gives $\frac{0}{0}$. Apply L'Hôpital's rule again:
>    $$
>    \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^x}{e^x + (e^x + x e^x)} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^x}{2e^x + x e^x}
>    $$
> 5. Substitute $x = 0$:
>    $$
>    \frac{e^0}{2e^0 + 0 \cdot e^0} = \frac{1}{2}
>    $$

### Exercise 8: Critical Points with Fractional Exponents
**Problem:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> Find the critical points of $f(x) = x^{2/3}(6 - x)^{1/3}$.

**Solution:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> 1. The domain of $f$ is all of $\mathbb{R}$ since the exponents have odd denominators (cube roots).
> 2. Compute the derivative using the product and chain rules:
>    $$
>    f'(x) = \frac{2}{3}x^{-1/3}(6-x)^{1/3} + x^{2/3}\left(\frac{1}{3}(6-x)^{-2/3}(-1)\right)
>    $$
>    $$
>    f'(x) = \frac{2(6-x)^{1/3}}{3x^{1/3}} - \frac{x^{2/3}}{3(6-x)^{2/3}}
>    $$
> 3. Combine the fractions:
>    $$
>    f'(x) = \frac{2(6-x) - x}{3x^{1/3}(6-x)^{2/3}} = \frac{12 - 3x}{3x^{1/3}(6-x)^{2/3}} = \frac{4 - x}{x^{1/3}(6-x)^{2/3}}
>    $$
> 4. Find where the derivative is zero or undefined:
>    - $f'(x) = 0 \implies 4 - x = 0 \implies x = 4$.
>    - $f'(x)$ is undefined when the denominator is zero, i.e. at $x = 0$ and $x = 6$.
> 5. The critical points are $x = 0, 4, 6$.

## Exam Tip: Checking Indeterminate Forms

When applying L'Hôpital's rule, **do not forget** to verify that the limit is indeed of the form $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ before differentiating.
*Example:* If you have the limit $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x + 5}{x + 2}$ and differentiate numerator and denominator, you will incorrectly find $\lim \frac{1}{1} = 1$. However, the actual limit by direct substitution is $\frac{5}{2}$.
In written exams, always write the phrase: *"Due to the indeterminate form $\left[\frac{0}{0}\right]$, we apply L'Hôpital's rule"* to show that you checked the prerequisites.
