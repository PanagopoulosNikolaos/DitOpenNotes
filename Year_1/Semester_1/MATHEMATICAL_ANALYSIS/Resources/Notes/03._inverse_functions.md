# Inverse Functions

The study of the concepts of monotonicity, "one-to-one" (1-1) functions, the horizontal line test, the definition and finding of the inverse function, and its geometric properties.

---

## 1. "One-to-One" (1-1) Functions

A function $f$ is "one-to-one" if it does not map two different elements of its domain to the same value.

### Definition of 1-1
A function $f$ with domain $A$ is called 1-1 if for any $x_1, x_2 \in A$:
$$
x_1 \neq x_2 \implies f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)
$$
Equivalently (via the contrapositive):
$$
f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2
$$

### Horizontal Line Test
A function $f$ is 1-1 if and only if no horizontal line intersects its graph at more than one point.
- If a horizontal line $y = y_0$ intersects the graph at two points $x_1$ and $x_2$, then $f(x_1) = f(x_2) = y_0$ with $x_1 \neq x_2$, so the function is not 1-1.

---

## 2. Inverse Function

Only functions that are 1-1 have an inverse function.

### Definition of the Inverse
Let $f: A \to B$ be a 1-1 function with domain $A$ and range $B$. The inverse function of $f$, denoted by $f^{-1}$, has domain $B$, range $A$, and is defined by:
$$
f^{-1}(y) = x \iff f(x) = y
$$
for every $y \in B$.

### Cancellation Equations
Due to the definition of the inverse, the following identities hold:
$$
f^{-1}(f(x)) = x, \quad \forall x \in A
$$
$$
f(f^{-1}(y)) = y, \quad \forall y \in B
$$

### Geometric Property of the Graph
The graph of $f^{-1}$ is obtained by reflecting the graph of $f$ across the bisector of the first and third quadrants of the axes, i.e. the line:
$$
y = x
$$
This happens because the point $(a, b) \in G(f) \iff (b, a) \in G(f^{-1})$.

---

## 3. Method for Computing the Inverse Function

To find the formula for $f^{-1}$ algebraically:
1. Set $y = f(x)$.
2. Solve the equation for $x$ (if possible), expressing $x$ as a function of $y$: $x = f^{-1}(y)$.
3. Replace $y$ with $x$ to obtain the function in its usual form: $y = f^{-1}(x)$.
4. Determine the domain of $f^{-1}$, which equals the range of $f$.

## Solved Exercises

### Exercise 1: Proving 1-1 and Finding the Inverse
**Problem:**
Show that the function $f(x) = x^3$ is 1-1 and find its inverse.

**Solution:**
1. **Proof of 1-1:** Let $x_1, x_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ with:
   $$
   f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1^3 = x_2^3
   $$
   Taking the cube root of both sides (which preserves the sign):
   $$
   x_1 = x_2
   $$
   Therefore $f$ is 1-1.
2. **Finding the inverse:** Set $y = x^3$ and solve for $x$:
   $$
   x = \sqrt[3]{y}
   $$
3. Changing the variable:
   $$
   f^{-1}(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}
   $$
   with domain $D(f^{-1}) = \mathbb{R}$.

### Exercise 2: Inverse of a Rational Function
**Problem:**
Find the inverse of the function $f(x) = \frac{2x + 3}{x - 1}$ on its domain.

**Solution:**
1. The domain of $f$ is $D(f) = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$.
2. Set $y = \frac{2x + 3}{x - 1}$ and solve for $x$:
   $$
   y(x - 1) = 2x + 3 \implies yx - y = 2x + 3 \implies yx - 2x = y + 3
   $$
   $$
   x(y - 2) = y + 3 \implies x = \frac{y + 3}{y - 2}
   $$
3. The solution is unique for $y \neq 2$, so the function is 1-1 and the inverse is:
   $$
   f^{-1}(x) = \frac{x + 3}{x - 2}
   $$
4. The domain of the inverse (which is the range of $f$) is:
   $$
   D(f^{-1}) = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{2\}
   $$

### Exercise 3: Restricting the Domain
**Problem:**
Given the function $f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 5$. Restrict its domain appropriately so that it becomes 1-1, and find its inverse on this interval.

**Solution:**
1. The graph of $f(x)$ is a parabola. We write the formula in completed square form:
   $$
   f(x) = (x^2 - 4x + 4) + 1 = (x - 2)^2 + 1
   $$
   The vertex of the parabola is at the point $(2, 1)$.
2. The function is not 1-1 on $\mathbb{R}$ (e.g. $f(1) = 2 = f(3)$). We restrict the domain to the right branch of the parabola, i.e. to the interval $A = [2, \infty)$, where the function is strictly increasing, hence 1-1.
3. Set $y = (x - 2)^2 + 1$ with $x \ge 2$ and $y \ge 1$. Solve for $x$:
   $$
   y - 1 = (x - 2)^2 \implies x - 2 = \sqrt{y - 1} \quad (\text{since } x - 2 \ge 0)
   $$
   $$
   x = 2 + \sqrt{y - 1}
   $$
4. Therefore, the inverse function is:
   $$
   f^{-1}(x) = 2 + \sqrt{x - 1}
   $$
   with domain $D(f^{-1}) = [1, \infty)$.

### Exercise 4: Inverse with a Radical
**Problem:**
Show that the function $f(x) = \sqrt{x - 2}$ is 1-1 and define $f^{-1}$.

**Solution:**
1. The domain of $f$ is $D(f) = [2, \infty)$.
2. **Proof of 1-1:** Let $x_1, x_2 \in [2, \infty)$ with:
   $$
   f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies \sqrt{x_1 - 2} = \sqrt{x_2 - 2}
   $$
   Squaring both sides:
   $$
   x_1 - 2 = x_2 - 2 \implies x_1 = x_2
   $$
   Therefore it is 1-1.
3. **Computing the inverse:** Set $y = \sqrt{x - 2}$ with $y \ge 0$.
   $$
   y^2 = x - 2 \implies x = y^2 + 2
   $$
4. Consequently:
   $$
   f^{-1}(x) = x^2 + 2
   $$
   with domain $D(f^{-1}) = [0, \infty)$ (which coincides with the range of $f$, since $y = \sqrt{x-2} \ge 0$).

### Exercise 5: Proving 1-1 via Monotonicity
**Problem:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> Show that the function $f(x) = x^5 + x^3 + x$ is 1-1 on $\mathbb{R}$.

**Solution:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> 1. We will use the property that every strictly monotonic function is 1-1.
> 2. Let $x_1, x_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ with $x_1 < x_2$.
> 3. By the properties of powers with odd exponents, we have:
>    $$
>    x_1 < x_2 \implies x_1^5 < x_2^5
>    $$
>    $$
>    x_1 < x_2 \implies x_1^3 < x_2^3
>    $$
> 4. Adding the three inequalities term by term:
>    $$
>    x_1^5 + x_1^3 + x_1 < x_2^5 + x_2^3 + x_2 \implies f(x_1) < f(x_2)
>    $$
> 5. Therefore, the function $f$ is strictly increasing on $\mathbb{R}$, hence it is 1-1.

### Exercise 6: Inverse of an Exponential Function
**Problem:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> Find the inverse of the function $f(x) = 1 - e^{-x}$.

**Solution:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> 1. The function is defined for every $x \in \mathbb{R}$.
> 2. Set $y = 1 - e^{-x}$. Solve for $x$:
>    $$
>    e^{-x} = 1 - y
>    $$
> 3. For the exponential expression to be defined, we require $1 - y > 0 \implies y < 1$.
> 4. Take the natural logarithm of both sides:
>    $$
>    -x = \ln(1 - y) \implies x = -\ln(1 - y)
>    $$
> 5. Changing the variable:
>    $$
>    f^{-1}(x) = -\ln(1 - x)
>    $$
>    with domain $D(f^{-1}) = (-\infty, 1)$.

### Exercise 7: Properties of Inverse Values
**Problem:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> Let $f$ be a 1-1 function for which $f(3) = 5$ and $f(5) = 3$. Compute the value of the expression $A = f^{-1}(5) + f(f^{-1}(3))$.

**Solution:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> 1. From the definition of the inverse function, we have:
>    $$
>    f(x) = y \iff f^{-1}(y) = x
>    $$
> 2. Since $f(3) = 5$, it follows that:
>    $$
>    f^{-1}(5) = 3
>    $$
> 3. Also, from the cancellation properties, for every $y$ in the range of $f$:
>    $$
>    f(f^{-1}(y)) = y
>    $$
>    Therefore, for $y = 3$:
>    $$
>    f(f^{-1}(3)) = 3
>    $$
> 4. We substitute the values into the expression $A$:
>    $$
>    A = 3 + 3 = 6
>    $$

### Exercise 8: Inverse of a Piecewise Function
**Problem:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> Find the inverse of the function:
> $$
> f(x) = \begin{cases} 
> x, & x < 1 \\
> x^2, & x \ge 1
> \end{cases}
> $$

**Solution:**
> **[Supplementary]**
> 1. We examine whether the function is 1-1 on each branch separately and whether the ranges of the branches are disjoint.
>    - For $x < 1$, the branch $y_1 = x$ produces the range $B_1 = (-\infty, 1)$.
>    - For $x \ge 1$, the branch $y_2 = x^2$ is strictly increasing and produces the range $B_2 = [1, \infty)$.
>    - Since $B_1$ and $B_2$ are disjoint ($B_1 \cap B_2 = \emptyset$) and the function is strictly increasing on each branch, $f$ is overall 1-1.
> 2. We find the inverse for each branch:
>    - For $y < 1$: $y = x \implies x = y$.
>    - For $y \ge 1$: $y = x^2 \implies x = \sqrt{y}$ (since $x \ge 1$).
> 3. We combine the branches to define the inverse function:
>    $$
>    f^{-1}(x) = \begin{cases} 
>    x, & x < 1 \\
>    \sqrt{x}, & x \ge 1
>    \end{cases}
>    $$
>    with domain $D(f^{-1}) = \mathbb{R}$.

## Exam Tip: Proving 1-1 via the Derivative

In exams, the classical method $f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$ can be extremely difficult algebraically. A much faster method is to use the derivative (provided the function is differentiable):
1. Compute the derivative $f'(x)$.
2. If $f'(x) > 0$ for every $x \in A$, then $f$ is strictly increasing, hence 1-1.
3. If $f'(x) < 0$ for every $x \in A$, then $f$ is strictly decreasing, hence 1-1.
*Warning:* If the derivative changes sign, the function is not 1-1 on all of $A$, but you can restrict it to intervals where the derivative maintains a constant sign.
